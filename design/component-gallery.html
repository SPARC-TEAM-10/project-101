Colour

Three families with one job each. Clay is the brand — primary actions, navigation, the hero panel, everything routine. Blood red is reserved: blood groups, emergency, donate actions, and destructive controls. Go green is narrower still — it is the control by which a person commits to help, and nothing else. Because red appears rarely it reads as a signal rather than decoration, and because green appears rarer still, a green button on this product means "I am coming." Warm sand carries all three. Measured contrast is shown where it matters.

go vs. leaf — not interchangeable

--go is the act of accepting: the button a donor presses to commit. --leaf is the state that follows, and every other confirmed status — verified facility, eligible donor, success toast. A green button that means "this worked" and a green button that means "I am coming" cannot be the same green.

Urgency

A three-step scale — neutral, amber, red — with three signals working together so no single one carries it: a left rail on the card, a labelled pill, and an elapsed-time ring that fills as the request ages over its 6-hour life. Red is the top of the scale only. If every card were red the scale would mean nothing.

The three labels are Emergency, Urgent and Standard. Not High, not Normal, not Critical — these exact words in every surface, including push notifications and SMS. A donor who reads "Urgent" in a text message and "High" in the app has to work out whether those are the same thing, at the one moment we need them not thinking about the interface.

Request lifetime — 6 hours

A request expires 6 hours after it is posted and stops accepting responses. The countdown is always labelled, always in tabular figures, and the label changes with the meaning — a bare number is never left sitting on a screen.

Expires in5:42Live, counting down from 6:00

Requests close 6 hours after they are posted. Donors can no longer respond, and everyone who accepted has been told.

Buttons

One primary per screen. Clay for routine actions, red for blood and emergency, green for accepting, outlined red for destructive — an outline signals consequence without shouting. Emergency paths use the 54px size, and loading holds the button's width so nothing below it moves. Click any of these; the toasts are live.

Accept and Decline are not a symmetric pair

Accept is the point of the screen: green, full width, 54px. Decline sits below it as a secondary at 50px — neutral, never red. Declining is a legitimate answer, not an error and not a destructive act, and two equally weighted filled buttons force a decision the donor has not been given enough information to make.

Accepting shares your name and number with the requester.

Off-app delivery — push and SMS

The same alert reaches donors three ways: in the Notification Center, as a push notification, and — for donors without the app or with push disabled — as an SMS. All three carry the same four facts in the same order: blood group and units, urgency, approximate distance, area. None of them ever carries the patient's name, the requester's identity, an exact address or a reason for the transfusion. These land on lock screens anyone can read.

SMS — the reply-keyword pattern
CHH: O− blood needed, 2 units. Emergency. About 5 km from you, Ernakulam. Reply YES to accept, NO to decline.
146 characters · 1 segmentNo link, no emoji, no branding line

Under 160 characters so it sends as one segment and cannot arrive out of order. Two keywords at most, uppercase in the copy and case-insensitive in handling. A YES is answered with the requester's name and number — the same reveal-on-acceptance rule as the app. A NO is acknowledged once and ends the thread.

What an alert may and may not say
Always

Blood group · units needed · urgency in one of the three words · approximate distance · area

Never

Patient name · requester name or number · exact address or map pin · diagnosis or reason · a distance precise enough to locate someone

Distance is always phrased as approximate — "about 5 km away". A distance to one decimal place is a location disclosure dressed up as a convenience.

Matched donors — who sees which donors

The most privacy-sensitive list in the product, and it differs by who is looking. A guest requester sees only donors who have reached Accepted — no pending rows, no locked placeholders, no per-donor trace. A registered requester sees every matched donor as Donor 1, Donor 2, Donor 3… in stable match order, each unaccepted row carrying the same flat Pending label — never Sent, Viewed or Declined, because delivery telemetry about a named individual invites exactly the pressure this product must not create. In both cases, accepting is what reveals a name and a number, and nothing before it does.
